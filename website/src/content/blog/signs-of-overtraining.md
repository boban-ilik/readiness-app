Your legs feel flat, your usual pace costs more than it should, and maybe your watch has started saying "Unbalanced" or "Overreaching". The natural move is to check the numbers first: resting heart rate up, HRV down, sleep worse. Those are the signs a watch can measure, and in the research they are the less reliable half. They move by small amounts, often inside day-to-day noise, and in controlled studies of overreached athletes they sometimes moved the "wrong" way.

The signs that carry the diagnosis are the ones only you notice. The [joint consensus statement of the European College of Sport Science and the American College of Sports Medicine](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23247672/) calls a drop in performance the only certain sign of overtraining, and defines the full syndrome as a sport-specific performance decline plus disturbed mood that persists despite weeks or months of recovery. No blood test, heart rate number or HRV reading confirms it on its own. A [systematic review of hormonal studies](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5541747/) found resting hormone levels mostly normal in affected athletes. The consensus statement says no marker in use meets all the criteria for general use, so doctors diagnose overtraining by ruling out other causes, often only in hindsight.

So if you are slower at the same effort, the effort feels harder and your mood has shifted, take it seriously whatever your watch says. If a week or two of genuinely lighter training brings you back, that was most likely functional overreaching, the short dip a hard block is supposed to cause. If it does not, see a doctor.

## The 10 signs, sorted by who can see them

| Sign | Can a watch measure it? | What studies found | How much to trust it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Slower or weaker at the same effort | No, you have to test it | The one sign the consensus statement calls certain | High; required for any diagnosis |
| The same workout feels harder | No | Described as an early sign of overreaching | High, especially with a performance drop |
| Persistent fatigue, heavy or sore legs | No | Rises with training load | High as a warning, not specific on its own |
| Mood change: irritable, flat, anxious, unmotivated | No | Shifts within days of hard training and tracks the load | High; self-reports like this beat objective measures in the research |
| Worse sleep | Yes | Modest drops in duration and efficiency, reversed by a taper | Moderate; consistent but small |
| Resting heart rate change | Yes | Up [about 4.5 bpm](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsm.2007.042200) on average after short overload blocks, unchanged after longer ones, lower in two overreaching studies | Low on its own; can go either way |
| HRV change | Yes | Largely unchanged or slightly higher in overreaching; lower in one case report | Low on its own; can go either way |
| Heart rate will not climb in hard efforts | Yes, if you repeat the same test | Maximal heart rate lower in almost all overreaching studies | Moderate, with a repeatable test |
| More colds | No | More common in overreached athletes in one study | Suggestive, not diagnostic |
| Less appetite, weight loss, missed periods | No (a scale shows weight) | Listed in symptom tables; no sizes | A reason to see a doctor, not a self-check item |

The figures are group averages from trained athletes, linked in the sections below. They show the scale of change to expect, not what your next morning will look like.

## Overreaching or overtraining? Recovery time decides

"Overtraining" gets used for three different states. The consensus statement separates them by how long performance takes to come back, not by how hard you trained or how bad you feel:

- **Functional overreaching** is a short-term performance dip after hard training. Performance returns within days to weeks, about two weeks in studies, and then improves.
- **Non-functional overreaching** is a longer dip, with fatigue and mood symptoms, that takes weeks to months to recover from. There is no improvement at the end, just lost training time.
- **Overtraining syndrome** is the same picture lasting months, possibly years, with other causes ruled out.

Treat those time frames as agreed definitions, not measurements. Overtraining syndrome cannot ethically be induced, so, as a [2004 review of the field](https://doi.org/10.2165/00007256-200434140-00003) pointed out, most research has studied overreached athletes, not overtrained ones. [Kreher and Schwartz's practical guide for clinicians](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3435910/) notes that some diagnose overtraining syndrome once a return to previous performance takes more than 14 to 21 days of rest, a working rule rather than a tested cutoff. The same guide says the full syndrome appears to be extremely rare, though exact figures are lacking. For a recreational athlete who feels flat after a few hard weeks, the realistic question is usually functional versus non-functional overreaching.

### What is stage 3 overtraining syndrome?

Some health sites, Cleveland Clinic's among them, describe three numbered stages, with stage 3 ("parasympathetic") marked by fatigue, depression, lost motivation and a resting heart rate under 60 beats per minute. The model mirrors a symptom table in the Kreher and Schwartz guide that splits "parasympathetic" symptoms, more common in aerobic sports, from "sympathetic" ones such as insomnia, irritability and a fast heart rate, more common in anaerobic sports. The consensus statement mentions that split once, in passing, and classifies by recovery time, not numbered stages.

The stage model's heart-rate figures, over 100 and under 60 beats per minute at rest, mark the edges of the [normal adult resting range](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/003399.htm), not overtraining thresholds. MedlinePlus gives 60 to 100 beats per minute as normal for adults and 40 to 60 for well-trained athletes, so a resting heart rate under 60 is ordinary in trained people.

### How common is it?

The figures come from self-report surveys, not diagnosed cases. The [consensus statement](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23247672/) gives about 10% over a training season (range 7 to 21%) in collegiate swimmers and other endurance athletes. Career rates reach 60% of female and 64% of male elite runners, against 33% of nonelite adult runners. In a [survey of 376 young English athletes](https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0b013e318207f87b), 29% reported at least one episode. Beyond that one nonelite runner figure, there is no good number for recreational adults.

## Signs your watch can see

Wearables measure four relevant things: resting heart rate, HRV, sleep and heart rate during exercise. None moves reliably enough, or far enough, to diagnose anything on its own.

### Resting heart rate: a few beats, sometimes, in either direction

The usual advice says resting heart rate goes up. A [2008 meta-analysis of overload studies](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsm.2007.042200) found that it did after overload blocks of under two weeks but not after longer blocks, and warned that changes this small may fall within normal day-to-day variation. The average rise after short blocks was about 4.5 bpm, according to the [consensus statement's summary](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23247672/) of that analysis. The consensus statement also notes that resting heart rate changes are not consistently found in overtraining syndrome. In the triathlete study in the HRV section below, resting heart rate went down as the athletes overreached.

So the rule seen online, that a morning heart rate 10 bpm above normal means you have not recovered, has no basis in this research. In short morning recordings in athletes, [ordinary day-to-day noise in resting heart rate is about 10%](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3936188/), according to Martin Buchheit's review of heart-rate monitoring. At a resting heart rate of 50, that is about 5 bpm, roughly the size of the average overload effect.

### HRV: the most watched number, and it often rises

HRV is expected to fall when you overreach. The evidence does not back that up. A [2016 meta-analysis of endurance-trained athletes](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-016-0484-2) concluded that resting HRV is "largely unaffected by overreaching". In studies where performance fell, it found a small increase in resting RMSSD, the beat-to-beat measure Garmin reports (Apple Watch reports a different one, SDNN).

Two controlled studies show the same direction:

- In [21 trained male triathletes](https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0b013e3182980125), all 13 who did three weeks of overload became functionally overreached, losing 9.0% of their running test performance. Their weekly-averaged HRV rose and resting heart rate fell, both reversing in the taper. Once-a-week readings missed the change.
- In [12 male cyclists](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00421-017-3549-5), two weeks of heavy training very likely lowered time-trial performance while RMSSD likely rose, then fell back during the taper.

The opposite pattern exists, on thinner evidence. In a [case comparison of two elite triathletes](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00421-012-2354-4), the one diagnosed with non-functional overreaching showed a falling seven-day HRV average, and shrinking day-to-day variation, in the run-up to a poor race. That is a single athlete.

An HRV that is up, flat or down therefore does not settle the question. A low reading also has plenty of causes unrelated to training load; [what pulls HRV down, and by how much](https://thereadiness.app/blog/how-to-increase-hrv/) covers alcohol, illness and lost sleep.

### Sleep: worse, modestly, and reversible

Sleep held its expected direction better than resting heart rate or HRV: it got worse in both controlled studies below. In a [study of 27 trained male triathletes](https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0000000000000177), the 9 who became functionally overreached slept 7.9% less (a little under 40 minutes of an 8-hour night), with sleep efficiency down 1.6%, and both recovered during the taper. Another 9 did the same overload without overreaching. Identical training lands differently on different people.

In [13 highly trained male cyclists](https://doi.org/10.1080/02640414.2015.1085589), nine days of intensified training dropped sleep efficiency from 83.1% to 77.8%, even though they spent more time in bed. More time in bed with less actual sleep is a pattern worth looking for in your data. If total sleep is the issue, [how much sleep recovery needs](https://thereadiness.app/blog/how-much-sleep-for-recovery/) covers the target.

### Heart rate during exercise: the most useful measured sign, if you test it the same way

Maximal heart rate falls in almost all overreaching studies, according to a [review of heart-rate monitoring](https://doi.org/10.2165/00007256-200333070-00004), while heart rate at submaximal effort is less consistent.

In a [study of 24 highly trained triathletes](https://doi.org/10.1152/japplphysiol.01254.2012), variations in heart rate and blood lactate were the two factors that best separated the overreached athletes from the rest, though the authors note that no single variable can detect overreaching. Exercise heart rate is also less noisy than resting values in [Buchheit's figures](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3936188/), about 3% day to day against about 10%. Your watch only captures this if you repeat a comparable session, which is step 1 of the self-check below.

### How to read the measured signs

Read them as weekly averages against your own baseline (your normal over recent weeks), not as single mornings. In Buchheit's review, links between HRV and performance in overreached athletes only showed up in the average of at least 3 to 4 days, and averaging four readings halves the noise. A shift in either direction tells you to check the felt signs; on its own it decides nothing.

The noise figures in this section come from 5 to 10 minute morning recordings in athletes. Overnight wearable readings use different windows and methods, so take them as a sense of scale, not your device's error.

## What your watch's labels mean

**Garmin HRV Status.** The [owner's manual](https://www8.garmin.com/manuals/webhelp/GUID-F41EAFB3-6CC9-42DE-9C6C-9E358DBB0671/EN-US/GUID-9282196F-D969-404D-B678-F48A13D8D0CB.html) defines the labels by your seven-day average against your own baseline range: Balanced is inside it, Unbalanced is above or below it, Low is well below it. So "Unbalanced" can mean your HRV is high. Garmin [says the status needs about three weeks of overnight wear](https://www.garmin.com/en-US/garmin-technology/health-science/hrv-status/) to become fully active, and that "mounting evidence" links abnormally high HRV relative to your baseline with functional overreach. That direction fits the studies above; the confidence is Garmin's.

**Garmin Training Status.** "Overreaching" here is a training-load label. Garmin's [description of the status levels](https://www8.garmin.com/manuals/webhelp/GUID-F41EAFB3-6CC9-42DE-9C6C-9E358DBB0671/EN-US/GUID-6F81BF5B-B49A-4506-95E2-0F4A04D8B319.html) says your load is "very high and counterproductive" and suggests adding lighter training. The status is built from trends in estimated VO2 max, acute load and HRV status, not from a performance test or your mood.

**Apple Watch.** Apple reports SDNN, a different HRV calculation, from short readings taken in the background (by default every four hours) and during Breathe sessions, according to [Apple's heart rate white paper](https://www.apple.com/health/pdf/Heart_Rate_Calorimetry_Activity_on_Apple_Watch_November_2024.pdf). Those numbers are not comparable with Garmin's overnight RMSSD. [What a good HRV looks like on each device](https://thereadiness.app/blog/what-is-a-good-hrv/) explains the differences, and if you wear a Garmin with an iPhone, [getting Garmin HRV into Apple Health](https://thereadiness.app/blog/garmin-hrv-apple-health/) covers the sync gap.

None of these labels is the consensus diagnosis. Treat them as a prompt to run the self-check.

## Signs only you notice

**Performance and effort.** This is the sign a diagnosis needs. The Kreher and Schwartz guide adds two early clues: being able to start a session but not finish it, or losing your finishing kick, and a higher perceived effort for the same workload.

**Fatigue, vigor and mood.** Mood moves fast. According to the [consensus statement](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23247672/), as few as 2 days of intensified training can raise scores on a standard mood questionnaire (the Profile of Mood States), ahead of markers such as cortisol. In studies covering more than 1,000 athletes, mood followed training load in a dose-response pattern, and self-reported muscle soreness and "heaviness" follow the same pattern. A [study that monitored competitive swimmers over ten years](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1478455/) found mood disturbance rose with training load and fell back to baseline when the load came down.

A [systematic review of studies that measured both](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4789708/) compared self-reported measures with objective ones such as heart rate and blood markers. Subjective measures were more sensitive and consistent in 22 of 54 studies, and where the two differed, 85% favoured the subjective measure. Objective measures were generally unresponsive to short-term changes in load, while vigor and motivation were among the self-reports that did respond.

How you feel is not the soft part of the evidence. It is the more sensitive detector.

**Anxiety and depression.** Can overtraining cause anxiety? It can come with it. Anxiety appears in the Kreher and Schwartz symptom table, and mood disturbance is part of the definition of overtraining syndrome. Per the consensus statement, depression rises the least of the mood measures during heavy overload but the most in athletes showing signs of overtraining syndrome. Anxiety and low mood have many causes; if they persist, talk to a doctor or mental health professional, whatever your training log says.

**Colds.** In the [triathlete sleep study](https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0000000000000177), upper respiratory infections were reported by 67% of the overreached athletes, 22% of those who trained just as hard without overreaching, and 11% of the controls. The wider picture is calmer: a [position statement from the International Society of Exercise and Immunology](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21446352/) found that most elite athletes get respiratory symptoms at about the same rate as the general population, with episodes clustering around competitions and, in illness-prone athletes, after increases in load. Frequent colds in a hard block are a reason to back off, not proof of overtraining.

**Appetite, weight and periods.** Appetite and weight loss appear in symptom tables, and the consensus statement notes that athletes in hard training tend not to raise food intake to match. Losing weight without trying, eating less than your training needs, or a lost period falls under what the International Olympic Committee calls [Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport (REDs)](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2023-106994): health and performance effects of too little energy for the training load, in women and men. That needs a clinician, not a self-check.

## A self-check before you decide anything

Work through it in order. It takes one to two weeks and sorts likely explanations; it does not diagnose.

1. **Test performance at a fixed effort.** Repeat a session you know well in similar conditions: the same route at the same heart rate, or the same pace or power. Slower at the same heart rate, unable to reach your usual heart rate, or a clearly harder effort all point the same way.
2. **Run the consensus checklist.** It opens with four items: unexplained underperformance, persistent fatigue, an increased sense of effort in training, and sleep problems. Add mood. Then rule out other explanations, such as a recent illness, eating less, work or family stress, travel or alcohol, before training takes the blame.
3. **Read weekly averages.** Compare the last seven days of HRV and resting heart rate with your baseline. A clear shift in either direction adds weight to steps 1 and 2. No shift does not clear you.
4. **Cut back for one to two weeks, then retest.** Reduce training, or rest completely if you feel unwell, then repeat the step 1 session.

Then take the branch that fits:

- **Back to normal or better within about two weeks.** That fits functional overreaching: the block worked and recovery was the missing piece. Resume, with more recovery built into the next block.
- **Still flat or tired after one to two weeks of real rest.** US government patient guidance from [MedlinePlus](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/patientinstructions/000807.htm) says to see your health care provider at this point, and that you may need to keep resting or dial back training for a month or longer. Follow the recovery plan and the doctor section below.

Do not wait out the two weeks if you have signs of infection, unexplained weight loss, a lost period, or low mood that is not lifting. Those go straight to the doctor section.

## How long recovery takes, and how to recover

| State | Typical recovery time | How solid that is |
|---|---|---|
| Functional overreaching | Days to weeks; about 2 weeks in studies, then performance improves | Best supported; seen directly in controlled overload studies with a taper, like the triathlete and cyclist studies above |
| Non-functional overreaching | Weeks to months | Consensus definition; few prospective data |
| Overtraining syndrome | Months, possibly years | Consensus definition; very little data, because it cannot be induced ethically, and usually diagnosed in hindsight |

Use them to set expectations; none of them predicts your own return date.

### How to recover from overtraining

1. **Cut the load or rest.** The consensus statement says reduced training may be enough in some cases of overreaching, and that overtraining syndrome is treated with rest; the Kreher and Schwartz guide notes that some prefer relative rest (easy movement). The cut percentages quoted on some health sites are rules of thumb without a cited source.
2. **Eat enough, especially carbohydrate.** In a cyclist study described in the [consensus statement](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23247672/), a high-carbohydrate diet during intensified training reduced the severity of overreaching. After two weeks of lighter training, performance was above baseline on that diet but still below it on the lower-carbohydrate one. In the cyclist sleep study above, mood disturbance was also smaller on high carbohydrate. Since appetite does not reliably rise with training, eating enough may take deliberate effort.
3. **Protect sleep.** It got worse in both overload studies above and recovered with the taper.
4. **Trim other stress.** The consensus statement lists sleep loss, work pressure, moving house, and relationship or family difficulties as stressors that add to training.
5. **Come back gradually, volume before intensity.** For non-functional overreaching and overtraining syndrome, the [Kreher and Schwartz guide](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3435910/) recommends building volume before intensity, starting with 5 to 10 minutes a day until an hour is tolerated. The consensus statement says the return should be individualized "because there is no definitive indicator of recovery". A practical test follows from that: you are back when your step 1 benchmark and your mood are back, not just your HRV.

Once you are training normally again, the daily question becomes whether to push or hold back this morning. [Training by HRV](https://thereadiness.app/blog/hrv-training/) covers that decision.

### How to avoid overtraining

The Kreher and Schwartz guide is frank that there is no evidence-based way to prevent overtraining syndrome. The consensus statement advises:

- Keep a daily training log that records how you feel, not just what you did.
- When performance drops or fatigue is excessive, cut that day's training or take a full rest day.
- Avoid monotony; vary hard and easy.
- Count work, family and sleep problems as part of your training stress.

Mood tracking has a striking result behind it: in one study cited by the [Kreher and Schwartz guide](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3435910/), adjusting collegiate swimmers' training load to their mood questionnaire scores cut burnout from 10% to zero.

## When to see a doctor

See a doctor if:

- you are still tired or underperforming after one to two weeks of genuine rest;
- you have signs of an infection that is not clearing (meanwhile, the consensus advice is to stop or reduce training while ill);
- you are losing weight without trying, eating less than you train, or have lost your period;
- low mood, anxiety or loss of interest is not lifting.

Because the diagnosis works by exclusion, a doctor will look for other causes of underperformance. The consensus statement lists anemia and iron deficiency, thyroid and adrenal disorders, diabetes, infections including glandular fever, and eating disorders. That is a reason to go, not a list to diagnose yourself from.

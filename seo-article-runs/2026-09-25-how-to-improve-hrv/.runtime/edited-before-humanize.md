Your HRV has been sliding for a week, or it has always sat lower than you think it should, and every list you find says the same things: sleep, exercise, breathe, drink water. The short answer is to stop the dips first, build the baseline second, and judge the result with weekly averages rather than single mornings. The reason most lists feel unhelpful is that they mix three different kinds of change, and the one you probably want, a higher baseline, is the slowest of the three.

The dips are where the fast, measurable wins are, and alcohol is the biggest of them. In [a study of 4,098 Finnish employees](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5878366/) whose nights were compared with their own alcohol-free nights, RMSSD (the HRV calculation most wearables other than Apple Watch report) in the first three hours of sleep was about 2 ms lower after a low dose, 6 ms lower after a moderate dose and 13 ms lower after a high dose. If your number is 8 ms down this morning and you had three drinks last night, you probably do not need a new supplement.

## Three ways HRV moves

- **Dips** pull the number down for a night or a few days: alcohol, illness, lost sleep, a hard session, acute stress, and the second half of the menstrual cycle.
- **Baseline changes** move your normal over weeks: consistent training, and weight loss if you carry excess weight.
- **In-session effects** raise HRV only while you do something or shortly after: slow breathing, cold water after exercise.

The plan follows from that. Clear out the dips, because they are quick and well measured. Pick one or two baseline changes and give them weeks to months, which is what the trial lengths suggest rather than a promise. Use in-session tools for what they are good at, without mistaking them for progress. Then check with numbers.

| Lever | Kind of change | Size or time frame | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Alcohol | Dip, overnight | About 2, 6 and 13 ms lower RMSSD in the first 3 hours of sleep (low, moderate, high dose) | Strong |
| Illness | Dip, days | About 10% lower on average | Strong (observational) |
| Hard session | Dip, 24 to 48 hours | About 4.6% change on average with training | Moderate to strong |
| Lost sleep | Dip | RMSSD lower after sleep deprivation; no single size | Moderate |
| Acute stress | Dip | Lower; no single size | Moderate |
| Menstrual cycle | Predictable swing | About 3.2% between follicular and luteal phases | Moderate (context, not a lever) |
| Consistent training | Baseline, over weeks | Higher in trials of 4 or more weeks; no millisecond estimate | Moderate |
| Weight loss, if overweight | Baseline | Most of 12 studies found a benefit; no pooled size | Weak to moderate |
| Slow breathing | In-session | Higher during and just after; no overnight change after a month in one trial | Strong in-session, null overnight |
| Cold water after exercise | In-session | Faster rebound after exercise; waking HRV unchanged | Moderate in-session, null on waking |
| Hydration | Unclear | Two small daytime heat trials | Weak |
| Fish oil | None on RMSSD or SDNN | 15 trials pooled | Null |

The sizes are group averages from different methods and populations, sourced in the steps below. Use them to judge scale, not to predict your next night.

## Step 1: Check that it is really low for you

Before changing anything, make sure the low number is low for you. That means the same device, the same measurement window (overnight, for most wearables) and your own baseline, not an age chart or a friend's watch. If you want the age ranges and what each device actually reports, [what counts as a good HRV](https://thereadiness.app/blog/what-is-a-good-hrv/) covers both. This article does not repeat them.

A baseline needs time. A [2025 review of HRV monitoring with mobile devices](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12787763/) recommends collecting daily readings for at least 7 consecutive days under consistent conditions before you judge anything against them. If you do not have a week of readings yet, that is the first job.

Then allow for ordinary wobble. In short morning recordings in athletes, [the day-to-day variation of RMSSD](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3936188/) was about 12%. As a rough illustration, that is about 6 ms on a 50 ms baseline and about 3.6 ms on a 30 ms one. Your own overnight wobble may be larger or smaller, and the same review is clear that no threshold for a meaningful change has been validated. Still, a 4 ms dip on a 50 ms baseline sits inside that ordinary wobble.

This step is finished when you know your baseline from at least a week of readings and can say whether this week's average, not this morning, sits below it by more than a normal wobble.

## Step 2: Remove what pulls it down

Start with the ordinary causes. HRV is, in the words of [an analysis of 9 million morning readings](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8659706/), a "sensitive but not specific" marker of stress: it tells you something loaded your system, not what. These are the usual suspects, roughly in order of size and evidence.

### Alcohol (dip, strong evidence)

The Finnish figures above (about 2, 6 and 13 ms) come from dose groups of up to 0.25, 0.25 to 0.75, and over 0.75 grams of alcohol per kilogram of body weight. Converted approximately, and assuming a US standard drink of 14 g of pure alcohol, that is up to about one drink, about one to four drinks, and more than about four drinks for a 70 kg (about 154 lb) person. Heavier people need more to reach the same dose, lighter people less. Being fit did not protect: the effect was similar in physically active and sedentary people, and stronger in younger ones.

A second, larger dataset points the same way. In [5.1 million nights from 20,968 WHOOP members](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12970902/), a night with one drink more than a person's own average, compared with one drink fewer, came with HRV about 3.3 ms lower in men and 3.8 ms lower in women, and resting heart rate about 2.4 to 2.8 beats per minute higher. The data are WHOOP's own and some of the authors are affiliated with the company, so treat the exact sizes with some caution. The practical finding is useful anyway: drinking earlier in the day and getting more sleep after drinking each reduced the effect.

The action is simple: fewer drinks, fewer drinking nights, and earlier rather than late. If you drink on several nights a week, this is probably the fastest way to a higher weekly average, because you are removing low nights rather than waiting for your normal to climb.

### Illness (dip, strong observational evidence)

In the 9-million-reading dataset, HRV was about 10% lower during sickness. A Fitbit analysis of people with COVID-19, published as a preprint, described the usual signature: HRV down, heart rate and breathing rate up. Alcohol produces a similar HRV and heart-rate pattern, so rule out last night's drinks first. If neither explains it and you feel off, rest. The number is a symptom here, not a target.

### Lost sleep (dip, moderate evidence)

A 2025 meta-analysis of 11 sleep-deprivation studies with 549 participants found RMSSD fell significantly after sleep loss. SDNN, the calculation Apple Watch reports, also fell, but not significantly. The research gathered for this article found no trial showing that sleeping more than you need raises HRV above your baseline, so the supported advice is to stop losing sleep rather than to chase extra hours. The guideline to aim for, 7 or more hours for adults, is covered in [how much sleep recovery takes](https://thereadiness.app/blog/how-much-sleep-for-recovery/).

### Hard sessions (dip, moderate to strong evidence)

Intense exercise lowers vagal HRV, the part that reflects the recovery side of your nervous system, for about 24 to 48 hours. In the 9-million-reading data, training days came with about a 4.6% change on average. That dip is the cost of the session, not a problem, so do not stop training hard to protect the number. Just do not judge your baseline from the two mornings after your hardest day. Deciding what to train on a low morning is its own question, and the guide linked at the end of this article answers it.

### Acute stress (dip, moderate evidence)

A review of 37 publications found that HRV changed under induced stress in most studies, most often through lower parasympathetic activity, that same recovery side. A deadline week can show up as a run of lower nights. What that review does not show is that a stress-management programme will lift your baseline above where it sits in calm weeks. Removing the stressor removes the dip; beyond that, the evidence here is silent.

### The menstrual cycle (predictable swing, context rather than a lever)

Vagal HRV is lower in the luteal phase, the second half of the cycle, than in the follicular phase, and higher-than-usual progesterone predicted lower-than-usual HRV within the same person. The free-living data put the average swing at about 3.2%. There is nothing to fix here. It is a reason not to read a luteal-phase week as failure.

Give this step two weeks. By then you should be able to name the likely cause of each low night, and the dips you control (drinks, lost sleep, piled-up hard days) should be shrinking or gone from your weekly chart.

## Step 3: Build the baseline over weeks

This is the slow part. Of the levers covered here, consistent training is the best-supported way to raise a resting baseline, and even that support is moderate, not strong.

A [meta-analysis of 16 randomised trials with 623 healthy adults](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11250637/) found that training programmes of 4 or more weeks raised both SDNN and RMSSD. Three limits matter. The results are standardised effect sizes, so there is no millisecond figure to expect. Most recordings were short daytime ECGs, not overnight wearable readings. And the authors note that sex, age and type of exercise may change the effect. An older meta-analysis found that older people's heart rate responded significantly less to training, with a similar trend for HRV, and a larger 2025 analysis of 34 long-term trials with 1,434 participants found a clear pooled change only in the LF/HF ratio, a different HRV measure, while warning that the studies varied too much for confident conclusions. Some trials suggest interval training and strength training can raise resting HRV too, but not firmly enough to crown one type.

So the practical rule, and it is a judgment rather than a trial result: pick training you can sustain for months instead of hunting for the best session type. The trials put people on structured programmes. If you already train consistently, they say less about what adding more will do.

**Weight loss (baseline, weak to moderate evidence)** applies only if you carry excess weight. A systematic review of 12 studies in adults with overweight or obesity found that most showed better HRV after lifestyle weight loss, mostly through diet. No pooled size was reported, and nothing here supports weight loss as an HRV lever for someone already lean.

As for how long: trials that found an effect ran at least four weeks, and the 2025 analysis saw larger effects in programmes of eight weeks or more. Weeks to months is a fair reading of those trial lengths. It is an interpretation, not a promise, and no study here tells you how many milliseconds to expect.

One caution for people who train a lot. In elite endurance athletes, HRV has been seen to fall while fitness rose, and to rise during overtraining. That research is about elite athletes, so it does not mean your low number is fitness in disguise. It does mean more training is not a straight line to a higher number.

Before you move on, you should have picked one or two baseline changes you can keep for at least four weeks, without changing five things at once, and you should expect the result to show up as a higher four-week average rather than a higher single morning.

## Step 4: Use breathing and cold water for what they do

Both of these make HRV jump. Neither has been shown to raise the overnight or waking number your baseline is built from.

**Slow breathing (in-session, strong evidence; overnight, null in one trial).** Breathing at around six breaths a minute raises HRV while you do it and just after. A 2022 meta-analysis of 223 studies found higher vagal HRV during slow breathing, immediately after it, and after multi-session programmes, though that analysis does not answer whether a programme shows up in the overnight reading your watch takes. One trial tested exactly that. In [a randomised study published in Cell Reports Medicine](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9873947/), people did five minutes a day of cyclic sighing, box breathing, cyclic hyperventilation with retention or mindfulness meditation for a month while wearing WHOOP bands. The authors reported no significant change in HRV or resting heart rate in any group. The positive findings were in mood and respiratory rate. The senior author was Andrew Huberman, whose video on the topic presents long exhales and the physiological sigh as ways to raise HRV. The two fit together once you separate the rise during the breathing from the overnight number.

So breathing is worth doing for what it does: mood improved in that trial, it costs nothing, and few adverse effects are expected. HRV biofeedback has its own meta-analysis, and the effects it reports are on anxiety, depression, anger and performance, not on a resting HRV number. Just do not expect a month of breathwork to lift your overnight average.

**An Apple Watch caveat.** [Apple's heart rate white paper](https://www.apple.com/health/pdf/Heart_Rate_Calorimetry_Activity_on_Apple_Watch_November_2024.pdf) says the watch measures heart rate during a Breathe session in the Mindfulness app and publishes an HRV estimate from it to HealthKit, where it sits alongside the background samples. If you look at Apple Health and see HRV rise on days you use Breathe, part of that rise may be the breathing session itself.

**Cold water after exercise (in-session, moderate evidence; waking, null).** A meta-analysis of post-exercise recovery techniques found cold water immersion had a moderate to large effect on how fast vagal HRV recovered after exercise. In a crossover study of 11 cyclists, RMSSD straight after cold water was 144% higher than after passive rest, but [waking HRV did not differ](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00421-012-2445-2) between the two. One small study of 12 well-trained male runners found lower RMSSD during the first deep-sleep period after whole-body immersion than after partial immersion or none. If you like cold plunges, keep them for how they make you feel, not as an HRV hack.

The check for this step: you know which of your readings were taken during breathing or straight after cold water, and you leave them out when you judge your baseline.

## Step 5: Skip what does not work, or is oversold

Some popular advice fails on the evidence, and some rests on studies that measured something narrower than the advice.

- **Fish oil (null for time-domain HRV).** A [meta-analysis of 15 fish-oil trials](https://doi.org/10.3945/ajcn.112.049833) found no significant change in SDNN or RMSSD, the numbers wearables show. It did find a small rise in high-frequency power, a different, frequency-based measure.
- **Caffeine before exercise (null, narrow scope).** Doses of 2.1 to 6.0 mg per kilogram before exercise did not change post-exercise RMSSD recovery. Evening caffeine and overnight HRV were not part of that research.
- **Weighted blankets, leafy greens and water (oversold).** Polar's guide links a study behind several of its tips, which at least makes them checkable. Three of those studies measured something narrower. The weighted-blanket study applied deep pressure during wisdom tooth extraction and measured HRV during and after surgery, with no bedding and no sleep involved. The leafy-greens study was observational, in 586 older men, found an association with one spectral HRV measure, and found no association for other fruit and vegetables, fish or omega-3. The hydration trials kept people at 30 °C for four hours with or without two glasses of water, and those without lost about 0.6% of body weight. They measured daytime HRV in 12 and 56 participants, which is weak evidence for anything your watch records overnight.

Other supplements, such as magnesium or ashwagandha, were not reviewed to the same standard for this article, so it makes no claim about them either way.

You can move on when nothing in your plan depends on a supplement or gadget sold as an HRV booster, and anything you keep from this list, you keep for its own sake.

## What you cannot change, and why fit people can read low

Some of your number was never up for negotiation.

**Age.** In a large Dutch cohort, RMSSD fell steadily from young adulthood until about 60 and then held stable. In the 9-million-reading dataset, older age went with lower HRV at a moderate strength (a correlation of 0.35), while physical activity level was only weakly related (0.21).

**Genetics.** [In 149,067 people with 10-second ECGs](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7480943/), about 18% of the variation in RMSSD was heritable. In a study of 772 twins and siblings with 24-hour recordings, the genetic share of RMSSD was 40% to 48%. The estimate depends on how long the recording is, but either way a real share of why your number differs from someone else's comes from inheritance, not habits.

**Device.** Apple Watch reports SDNN, and devices differ in both calculation and measurement window, so comparing across devices tells you little. The good-HRV guide linked in Step 1 decodes what each one reports.

**"Why is my HRV so low even though I'm fit?"** Because fitness is not the main thing the number reflects. Age and inheritance set much of it, activity level was only weakly related to HRV across 28,175 app users, and in elite endurance athletes HRV can even fall as fitness rises. A low number on a fit person can simply be that person's number. Compare it with your own baseline, not with someone else's.

**"Can low HRV be reversed?"** Dips, yes, once the cause passes. The baseline, partly: training and, where relevant, weight loss can raise it over weeks to months, by an amount no study here can promise. Your set point, the share that comes from age and genes, no.

## Step 6: Check whether it worked

This step decides whether you keep a change or drop it, and it is where people give up on changes that were working or keep ones that were not. It needs four weeks and a few numbers.

1. **Write down your starting baseline.** Use the average of at least a week of readings before you change anything, same device, same window.
2. **Take at least 3 readings a week.** A study of trained triathletes concluded that [at least 3 valid readings per week](https://doi.org/10.1123/ijspp.2013-0455) are needed to stand in for a full week's average.
3. **Average each week.** Weekly averages smooth out the drinks, the hard days and the bad nights that single mornings are full of.
4. **Compare after four weeks, not before.** The trials that found training effects ran at least that long. A four-week average above your starting baseline, and still there the following weeks, is the pattern you are looking for. A single morning above it means nothing on its own.

**A worked example of one baseline method.** Readiness Coach, the app behind this site, uses a public convention that shows why this check needs patience. It keeps about 30 days of nights, drops the lowest 20%, takes the median of the rest as your baseline, and waits for 7 nights before using a personal baseline at all. Because the lowest nights are dropped and the rest are reduced to a median, one bad night cannot drag the baseline down, and a real improvement moves it only after it has shown up on many nights of the month. That is the product's convention, not a research finding; the [readiness score explainer](https://thereadiness.app/blog/what-is-a-readiness-score/) sets out the full score model. If you wear a Garmin, its HRV does not reach Apple Health on its own; the [Garmin to Apple Health workaround](https://thereadiness.app/blog/garmin-hrv-apple-health/) covers that.

Then branch on what you see.

**Nothing moved after 8 to 12 weeks.** Go back to Step 2 before you blame the training. If your weekly averages still include drinking nights, short nights or back-to-back hard days, the dips may be hiding a baseline that has moved. If they are clean and you have trained consistently, accept that some baselines barely move: older people responded less in the trials, results varied widely between studies, and part of the number is inherited. Eight to twelve weeks is a sensible point to reassess, not a research threshold.

**It rose, but you are sleeping badly or training very hard.** A higher number is not always good news. In highly trained endurance athletes, HRV has been seen to rise paradoxically during overtraining, so if the rise comes with poor sleep, unusual fatigue or stalled training, trust those signals over the number. On Apple Watch, also check whether the rise lines up with days you used Breathe.

**It fell and stayed down with nothing to explain it.** A clinical boundary, not a diagnosis. If your weekly averages sit well below your baseline for weeks with none of the Step 2 causes in play, or you feel unwell, see a doctor. The persistence matters more than any single value. HRV research in people with heart disease, such as [exercise therapy after a heart attack](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2903986/), is a separate field from this article, and if that applies to you, your doctor is the right person to set expectations. The good-HRV guide linked in Step 1 also has a section on when a low or high reading is worth attention.

You are done when you have four weekly averages from at least 3 readings each, compared with a baseline you recorded before you started, and you know which of the three branches you are on.

## Where to go next

Raising a baseline is a slow project with a short list: remove the dips you control, train consistently for weeks to months, and measure the result with weekly averages against your own normal. A number that jumps within days is more likely a dip ending, or a reading taken during a breathing session, than a new baseline.

Your Apple Watch runs watchOS 27, the release that introduced Apple's readiness score, and the Readiness app is nowhere on it. If the watch is a Series 9, 10 or 11, an Ultra 2 or 3, or an SE 3, nothing is broken and there is nothing to switch on. Apple's [Readiness user guide](https://support.apple.com/guide/watch/readiness-flx4gnzby346/watchos) says: "The Readiness app is available on Apple Watch Series 12 and Apple Watch Ultra 4 for ages 13 and older."

The better news is what your watch already has. Apple builds the score from activity, vitals and sleep, and every Series 6 or later, SE 2 or later and Ultra already shows a screen for each: Training load, the Vitals app and Sleep score. What it lacks is Apple's blend of the three into one number, and some of the measurements behind it.

Disclosure: this site makes Readiness Coach, one of the five apps compared below. No watch and no app was tested for this article. The facts come from Apple's pages and each vendor's listing or site, read on November 20, 2026.

## Which Apple Watch models get the Readiness app

Apple's US compare table covers 19 models. "Readiness app" appears for two of them, Apple Watch Series 12 and Apple Watch Ultra 4, and is blank for the other 17, as of November 2026. watchOS 27 is [compatible with six older models](https://support.apple.com/guide/watch/applewatch-models-apd2054d0d5b/watchos) too, so those six get the update without the app.

Two terms in the table: HRV, heart rate variability, is the variation in the time between heartbeats. Recovery HRV is the name Apple's table uses for HRV on the two new watches.

### If you have a Series 12 or Ultra 4 and see no score

The app is for ages 13 and older; the pages read do not say why. [Apple's support article on the score](https://support.apple.com/en-us/128112) lists four checks: height, weight and age entered; Activity set up; overnight vitals set up and the watch worn while sleeping for "at least 7 nights"; Sleep set up and the watch worn last night. Neither that article nor the user guide says the nights must be in a row.

## What Apple's readiness score is built from

Apple's user guide lists what goes into each of the three inputs:

- **Activity:** active calories, workout effort ratings and training load.
- **Vitals:** overnight heart rate, respiratory rate, wrist temperature, HRV and blood oxygen, plus daytime heart rate, compared with your recent baseline.
- **Sleep:** recent sleep score data.

The support article says which way they push. "Increased activity in general can reduce your score, while steady or reduced activity raises it." Vitals that read as off reduce it, and a low sleep score can too.

The result is a number from 0 to 10 in four bands: Go for it (8 to 10), Ready (5 to 7), Pace Yourself (2 to 4) and Recover (0 to 1). The guide says it "updates throughout the day based on calories burned, naps, and your daytime heart rate". Neither page says how much each input counts toward the number.

## What your older watch already shows, and what it lacks

Eleven older models have all three screens in Apple's compare table. The first-generation SE and the Series 5 and earlier have none of them.

**Sleep score.** A score out of 100 in the Sleep app. Apple's [sleep score guide](https://support.apple.com/guide/watch/view-your-sleep-score-apded441a669/27/watchos/27) says it is "calculated based on estimates of sleep duration (50 points), bedtime consistency (30 points), and interruptions (20 points)".

**Vitals.** [The Vitals app](https://support.apple.com/en-us/120142) compares overnight heart rate, respiratory rate and sleep duration, plus wrist temperature and blood oxygen on some models, with "your typical range": a range the watch establishes for each metric once you have worn it to sleep for 7 days.

**Training load.** In the Activity app. Apple's [training load guide](https://support.apple.com/guide/watch/track-your-training-load-apde4c07a6cf/27/watchos/27) says it "compares the intensity and duration of your workouts over the last 7 days to what you've done over the previous 28 days" and classifies the result from well below to well above.

**HRV.** Your watch records it and writes it to Apple Health, the store on your iPhone that the watch saves to and apps read from. On an older watch it is not one of the Vitals metrics, and Apple's guides to Sleep score and Training load do not mention it. [What counts as a good HRV](https://thereadiness.app/blog/what-is-a-good-hrv/) is a separate question.

Set against an Apple Watch Series 12 or Apple Watch Ultra 4, four things are missing: the blend into one number, HRV inside Vitals, the daytime view of Vitals, and higher-frequency heart rate and HRV sampling. The three screens do not cover every input either: Apple's activity group also counts active calories and workout effort ratings.

## Why Apple limits it, in Apple's words

Apple's stated reason is new sensor hardware in the Apple Watch Series 12 and Apple Watch Ultra 4, which it calls the Health Sensing System. Its [health announcement of September 9, 2026](https://www.apple.com/newsroom/2026/09/apple-advances-health-and-fitness-capabilities-using-apple-intelligence/) carries the subhead "The all-new Apple Watch Health Sensing System enables a new readiness score and higher-frequency HRV". The [Series 12 announcement](https://www.apple.com/newsroom/2026/09/introducing-apple-watch-series-12-with-the-all-new-health-sensing-system/) says that with "the new Health Sensing System and S11 chip", the watch "delivers higher-frequency heart rate and heart rate variability (HRV) measurements, which power an enhanced suite of health and fitness features including a new readiness score".

Apple says the new watches measure HRV "as often as every five minutes". For earlier watches, [Apple's November 2024 paper on heart rate measurement](https://www.apple.com/health/pdf/Heart_Rate_Calorimetry_Activity_on_Apple_Watch_November_2024.pdf) gives the default cadence of the background readings that HRV is calculated from as "every four hours". That paper predates watchOS 27, and the pages read for this article do not say whether the older cadence has changed.

Those pages also do not say whether a Series 11 or an Ultra 3 could run the Readiness app, or whether Apple plans to bring it to older watches. Apple gives a hardware reason for where the score lives. In the pages read, it does not call the score impossible elsewhere.

## Three ways to get a readiness read on an older watch

### Upgrade

Apple lists its own number for two watches, so getting it means buying one. In Apple's launch announcements of September 9, 2026, the Apple Watch Series 12 "starts at $399 (U.S.)" and the [Apple Watch Ultra 4](https://www.apple.com/newsroom/2026/09/apple-unveils-apple-watch-ultra-4/) "starts at $799 (U.S.)"; current store prices were not checked. A new watch will not show a score on day one: Apple asks for at least 7 nights of sleep with it first, and the age limit still applies. If you would consider a different device instead, this site compares [Whoop and Apple Watch](https://thereadiness.app/blog/whoop-vs-apple-watch/) separately.

### Read the three screens yourself

This method is this site's suggestion, built from Apple's descriptions of each screen. It is not Apple's advice. Each morning, look at your Sleep score, open Vitals to see whether anything sits outside your typical range, and check whether Training load sits toward the "well above" end of its scale. Apple says increased activity, off vitals and a low sleep score can each reduce its own score, so the more of the three that point that way, the stronger the case for an easier day.

It is free and needs no new app. It cannot give you one number, Apple's weighting or daytime updates, and it leaves out HRV, which sits in Apple Health. To bring HRV in, see this site's guide to [adjusting training on a low HRV day](https://thereadiness.app/blog/hrv-training/).

### Use a third-party app

An app can give you one number for the watch you own, but it is the app's number. [Apple's HealthKit documentation](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/healthkit/data-types), as read in November 2026, lists raw data types such as HRV, sleep stages and resting heart rate, and no type for Apple's readiness score, sleep score, vitals classification or training load. So an app has to compute its own score from the raw data in Apple Health.

On an older watch, that data is the older HRV measurement, not the Recovery HRV that Apple's table lists for the two new models. The same documentation does not say whether the HRV type Apple added in iOS 27 is Recovery HRV.

[Readiness Coach](https://apps.apple.com/app/id6760478506) is this site's own app. It is an iPhone app that reads Apple Health and scores overnight HRV and resting heart rate against your 30-day personal baseline, plus sleep and stress ([how the score is weighted](https://thereadiness.app/blog/what-is-a-readiness-score/)). One pattern in the table: three of the four third-party vendors describe a morning score, and Bevel's listing does not say. Apple's score updates through the day.

## How to choose

Start from the constraint that matters most:

- **It has to be Apple's number.** Upgrade to an Apple Watch Series 12 or Apple Watch Ultra 4.
- **It has to be free, with no new app.** Read the three screens.
- **You want one number for the watch you own.** Use an app, and check the "What is free" row first: the daily score is free in two of the five, and Unknown or unresolved in the other three.

One caution covers every option. Apple says its scoring algorithm "was developed using data from the Apple Heart and Movement Study". Development is not validation. No published validation of the score turned up in the Apple pages read for this article, and Apple's [Heart Rate Accuracy Study of September 2026](https://www.apple.com/health/pdf/Heart_Rate_Accuracy_Study_2026.pdf) names the Readiness app once and measures heart rate. This site's own app has no clinical validation study either.

So treat any of these numbers, Apple's included, as one input beside how you feel. Apple calls Readiness "a general wellness feature", and some [signs of overtraining are ones only you can notice](https://thereadiness.app/blog/signs-of-overtraining/).

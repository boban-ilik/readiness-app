## Quick Verdict

The Apple Watch now has a recovery score of its own. Apple's readiness score reached stores on September 18, 2026, rates the day ahead from 0 to 10, and answers with Recover, Pace Yourself, Ready, or Go For It. It runs on exactly two watches, the Series 12 and the Ultra 4. If you own either one, use it for two or three weeks before you pay anyone. If you own an older Apple Watch, readiness will not reach you, and Athlytic remains the cheapest honest route to WHOOP-style scoring at $4.99 a month or $29.99 a year. Bevel is the pick when your data is spread across several devices, at $14.99 a month or $99.99 a year.

The catch is that none of these apps measures anything. They read the same Apple Health records your watch already writes, then score them, so the question is never accuracy. It is whether a number changes what you do, and whether that is worth a subscription now running from $30 to $189 a year. Two things to check before you subscribe. Athletica does not name Apple Watch or Apple Health on its own integrations list, and Apple's newsroom releases and the watchOS user guide pages we checked do not say whether the Series 12's new Recovery HRV series is readable by other apps, so do not assume an app score improves on the new hardware.

## What Your Apple Watch Already Does in 2026

Start here, because two of the three jobs a recovery app used to do now sit in watchOS.

**Vitals.** Apple's Vitals app shows overnight heart rate, respiratory rate, wrist temperature, blood oxygen and sleep duration, and notifies you when several of them sit outside your typical range at once. On the Series 12 and the Ultra 4 you see heart rate variability instead of sleep duration, and daytime vitals take your heart rate and HRV into account.

**Training load.** Apple compares the intensity and duration of your workouts over the last 7 days with the previous 28 days, then places you on a scale from well below to well above.

**Readiness.** Apple's own score analyzes your recent activity, training load, vitals and sleep score. It arrived with the Series 12 at $399 and the Ultra 4 at $799, both in stores from September 18, 2026, and it depends on the new Health Sensing System, which Apple says measures HRV up to 24 times more often than before. Apple describes two HRV readings on that hardware, a Recovery HRV for daily stress and recovery signals and an overall HRV for cardiovascular trends. Our separate piece on [which Apple Watch models get readiness](/blog/apple-watch-readiness-which-models) covers the cutoff in detail.

What watchOS still does not give you is a multi-device view, a coach that explains itself, or your workout files in a format you can take elsewhere. That is what the apps below sell.

## Recovery Scoring: Athlytic, Bevel and Cora

### Athlytic, the WHOOP-style dashboard

Athlytic turns Apple Watch data into a daily Recovery score, an Exertion score and a Sleep score, with no extra hardware. Pro costs $4.99 a month or $29.99 a year, and Athlytic's own support page is blunt about the rest: there is no lifetime option and no discount, but there is a 7 day free trial. That is the number that moved since our last update, when this guide said about $24.99 a year.

Version 27, released on September 14, 2026, added Siri questions about recovery and training, and cycle tracking with phase based coaching.

Best for: owners of an Apple Watch older than the Series 12 who want WHOOP-style scoring without a subscription in the hundreds. Our [Athlytic vs WHOOP comparison](/blog/athlytic-vs-whoop-apple-watch-recovery) covers what you give up by not wearing the strap.

### Bevel, the multi-device aggregator

Bevel scores recovery, sleep and strain, adds nutrition logging, and pulls from more sources than the watch-only apps: its listing names Garmin, Oura and Google Health, including the Fitbit Air. Pro is $14.99 a month or $99.99 a year, the most expensive subscription here apart from Athletica's training plans, and its Bevel Intelligence features sit behind either that subscription or credit packs of $4.99 to $49.99.

Its App Store listing covers iPhone and Apple Watch, so if you or a training partner is shopping for Android, start with our [Bevel on Android](/blog/bevel-for-android) piece. See also our [Vora vs Bevel comparison](/blog/bevel-vs-vora-health-app-comparison).

Best for: people whose numbers come from a watch plus a ring or a strap.

### Cora, for a plain language read

Cora, from PurplePill AI, connects Apple Health and your existing wearable and reports recovery, Body Charge and strain in one place, then explains the numbers and ties them to your training. Its App Store listing shows two Premium purchases, $14.99 and $79.99, without naming the terms, and Cora's own site advertises a 7 day trial with full access. It reads as a coach rather than an instrument panel.

Best for: people who want their Apple Watch data interpreted rather than graphed.

## Training Plans and Exports: HealthFit and Athletica

### HealthFit, the workout analyst

HealthFit does not score recovery. It converts Apple Watch workouts into Training Load, Fitness (CTL), Fatigue (ATL) and Form (TSB), exports to FIT, GPX, CSV and Markdown, and syncs to more than 20 platforms including Strava, TrainingPeaks, Suunto, COROS and Intervals.icu. It is a $6.99 one time purchase with no subscription, which makes it by far the cheapest thing in this guide over two years, and it needs no account because the data stays on your device.

Best for: Apple Watch athletes who want their own files and the classic load metrics. Pair it with a scoring app rather than treating it as one.

### Athletica, with one caveat for Apple Watch owners

Athletica builds AI training plans for triathletes, rowers, cyclists and runners around training load and performance trends. It is also the most expensive option here at $19.90 a month, $99 for six months, or $189 a year.

The caveat matters in an Apple Watch guide. Athletica's own site lists Garmin, Strava, COROS, Wahoo and Concept 2 as the devices it syncs with, and names neither Apple Watch nor Apple Health, though the App Store listing's recent update notes do mention reading Apple Health. Until that is clearer, an Apple Watch owner should assume workouts arrive through Strava, and should confirm it during the trial before paying for a year.

Best for: endurance athletes who already push their data to Strava or train on a Garmin.

## Gentler Streak, for a Calmer Read

Gentler Streak builds an Activity Path from Apple Health data, tracks daily readiness, and suggests rest days instead of streaks. It syncs through Apple HealthKit, keeps data on the device, and does not require an Apple Watch. Premium is $8.99 a month or $39.99 a year, and unusually for this category there is a lifetime unlock from $59.99, which pays for itself inside two years.

Best for: people who want recovery context without a score that reads like a performance review.

## Do You Still Need a Recovery App?

If you bought a Series 12 or an Ultra 4, run Apple's readiness score for two or three weeks first. It costs nothing extra, it is built from the same inputs these apps use, and you may find that a 0 to 10 number plus Vitals notifications is all the guidance you act on.

Pay for an app when one of these is true. You own an older Apple Watch, so readiness will never appear, and you want a recovery number: start with Athlytic. Your data is spread across a watch, a ring and a strap: Bevel. You want your workout files and the classic load curves: HealthFit, once, for $6.99. You want structured endurance plans and you already use Strava or Garmin: Athletica. You want a calmer tone: Gentler Streak.

One honest note that applies to all of them. Every app here reads Apple Health, so a night when the watch was off the wrist produces a poor score in all of them equally. None of these apps can be more accurate than the sensor on your wrist.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does the Apple Watch have a recovery score?

It does now, on two models. Apple's readiness score analyzes your recent activity, training load, vitals and sleep score, rates the day from 0 to 10 and answers with Recover, Pace Yourself, Ready or Go For It. Apple's newsroom releases name it on the Apple Watch Series 12 and Ultra 4, which reached stores on September 18, 2026. On any older Apple Watch you still need a third-party app such as Athlytic, Bevel or Cora, or Vora, which we make.

### What is the best free Apple Watch recovery app?

On a Series 12 or Ultra 4 the free option is Apple's own readiness score, with no subscription. Otherwise most of these apps are free to download but put scoring behind a subscription: Athlytic gives you a 7 day trial and then charges $4.99 a month or $29.99 a year. Vora, which we make, is free to use with its Sleep, Recovery and Strain scores and 10 AI messages a day, with Pro at $12.99 a month or $89.99 a year.

### How much does Athlytic cost in 2026?

Athlytic is free to download, and Athlytic Pro is listed on the App Store at $4.99 a month or $29.99 a year as of September 18, 2026. Athlytic's own support page says there is a 7 day free trial, no lifetime purchase and no discount code.

### Does Athletica work with an Apple Watch?

Not directly, on the evidence Athletica publishes. Its own site lists Garmin, Strava, COROS, Wahoo and Concept 2 as the devices it syncs with, and does not name Apple Watch or Apple Health, although the App Store listing's update notes do mention reading Apple Health. Apple Watch owners should expect to route workouts through Strava and should test that during the free trial before paying $189 for a year.

### Do recovery apps make Apple Watch data more accurate?

No. They read the same Apple Health records your watch writes, then interpret them. A recovery app can give you a clearer decision, a longer trend or a multi-device view, but it cannot improve the measurement, so wearing the watch overnight matters more than which app you choose.

### Passage-grounded benchmark

**Garmin technology page (position 2; E1).** What it answers and how well:
- *What it is built from.* "The Body Battery feature works by continuously analyzing combinations of heart rate, heart rate variability (HRV) and movement data while you wear your device. The goal of this analysis is to identify meaningful physiological states and to describe the impact they have on your body's energy levels. At a high level, this means documenting if you are awake or asleep, when you are physically active and tracking stress levels during periods of inactivity." This is Garmin's clearest statement of the raw inputs. It does not give weights, a formula, or how the inputs are combined.
- *Stress and its link to HRV.* "Garmin all-day stress tracking continuously measures your physiological stress on a scale from 0 to 100. Stress levels below 25 are classified as rest, a state associated with parasympathetic dominance ... Stress levels above 25 reflect sympathetic dominance". A five-column table maps stress 0 to 25 to "Charging", parasympathetic, lower HR, higher HRV, and 25 to 100 to "Draining", sympathetic, higher HR, lower HRV (Garmin writes the ranges with dashes). Then: "How relaxed you are determines the strength of your Body Battery charge effect. A lower resting heart rate and higher HRV compared to your personal baseline are good indicators of effective resting." So Garmin does say a personal baseline is inside the charge logic; it does not say what that baseline is or show it for Body Battery.
- *Why it does not charge overnight.* "A good night's sleep is your ultimate recharge opportunity, although illness, alcohol consumption and residual stress can prevent you from fully charging overnight." One sentence, no mechanism and no sizes.
- *Sleep pressure.* A whole section explains that "your Body Battery is not automatically increasing just because you are resting" because accumulated sleep pressure drains it, and "a short night's sleep after a long day can mean the effects of accumulated sleep pressure will linger until you catch up". Useful and specific; the article should credit Garmin for this and not re-invent it.
- *Fitness.* "as your fitness level improves, exercise and stressful experiences have a smaller relative impact on your body ... This reality is built into the Body Battery feedback that your Garmin device provides." Another place where Garmin states a personalisation exists without showing it.
- *Low value.* "the occasional low-energy day is no cause for alarm ... However, if you find your Body Battery is perpetually low, it might be time to start looking for areas to improve." And: "being low on energy doesn't mean you can't exercise ... just because you can doesn't mean you should."
- *Attribution.* "Powered by the Garmin Human Performance Lab" (no Firstbeat mention on this page). Footnote 1 links Garmin's activity-tracking accuracy page.
- *Limits for the reader.* No numbers for the scale (the 5 to 100 range is on the manual page, not here); no mention of RMSSD, of how the watch handles a night not worn, of history views, of caffeine, or of Training Readiness. No formula or weights. The prose is long and marketing-toned ("When you have energy, use it."). This is the page the AI Overview summarises, so the article gains nothing by repeating its structure.

**Garmin support FAQ (position 6; E12).** What it answers and how well:
- *Inputs and scale.* "Using factors such as heart rate variability (HRV), stress, sleep, and activity levels, it provides an energy score ranging from 5 to 100." Same four inputs as the manual (E2); the raw signals (HR, HRV, movement) are on the technology page only. "To confirm that your device is compatible with this feature, refer to your owner's manual and search for 'Body Battery'."
- *Charged and Drained.* "In your Garmin Connect account, Body Battery includes Charged and Drained values ... Charged represents the amount of energy your body recovered. Drained represents the amount of energy your body used. Generally, these values should be relatively balanced over a 24-hour period". "The Charged and Drained metrics are calculated over a 24-hour period and reset at midnight. Garmin Connect may also provide personalized insights that highlight Body Battery changes since bedtime or since waking." This is the only Garmin page in the packet that names a Connect view in detail; it is a daily accounting, not a history range or a baseline band.
- *Low after sleeping.* "How rested you feel and your Body Battery score do not always align. Body Battery considers factors beyond sleep duration, including stress levels and your body's overall recovery." "Even if you sleep through the night, elevated stress levels during sleep can reduce how much your Body Battery recharges." Factor list: "Bright lights; Alcohol consumption; Excessive physical activity; Physical exhaustion; Extreme temperatures; Loud noises; Pain or discomfort; Illness; Frequent waking, tossing, or turning during sleep". This is Garmin saying, in its own words, that the number and the feeling can disagree, and why: the article's "when it misleads" section should open from this sentence rather than from an editorial claim. No sizes, no mechanism beyond "stress levels during sleep".
- *Stuck at 5 and the personal baseline.* "Body Battery requires a learning period to establish your personal baseline and provide accurate readings. If you recently started using a new Garmin device or performed a factory reset, this learning period typically lasts 5-7 days. During this time, your Body Battery may remain at or near 5". Recalibration: "If this baseline is lost, Body Battery may drain to 5 and remain there until enough physiological data is collected to reestablish it ... can take several days." So Garmin does state that Body Battery has a personal baseline and how long it takes to learn; it does not say what the baseline is, show it, or say how it enters the score. This materially narrows the intake's "no personal baseline" claim (§8a).
- *Device not worn.* "Body Battery can estimate charging and draining during short periods when the device is not worn. However, if the device is removed for several hours or longer, Body Battery will resume from a model-based estimate when it is worn again. For the most accurate results, wear your device as consistently as possible throughout the day and night". Direct support for the unworn-night case: the morning number after a night off the wrist is a model estimate, not a measurement.
- *Other devices and sync.* Data from non-Body-Battery devices is not included; "Body Battery TrueUp keeps Body Battery data in sync across multiple Garmin products"; a device list follows under "Which Models Keep Body Battery Data in Sync?". The list is scoped to TrueUp sync, and the page separately tells owners to check the manual for feature compatibility, so it should not be presented as "every watch with Body Battery" and it says nothing about Training Readiness.
- *Limits.* No formula, weights or baseline value; no history ranges beyond the daily Charged and Drained totals and the since-bedtime and since-waking insights; no RMSSD; no Training Readiness or HRV Status; no caffeine statement (that is on the manual page); no citations. Plain, well-organised support prose; the AI Overview and the PAA answers can lift it almost directly, which is why the article must add what it lacks (sizes, mechanism, the three-feature view, the published-formula contrast) rather than restate it.

**Garmin blog "5 reasons" (position 8; E3).** What it answers and how well:
- *Attribution and mechanism.* "The science behind Body Battery comes from Firstbeat's ability to interpret beat-to-beat changes in your heart also known as HRV status, to reveal interplay between the sympathetic ... and parasympathetic ... branches of your autonomic nervous system." This is the Firstbeat attribution the brief asked for, and it is in Garmin's own voice (the technology page says "Garmin Human Performance Lab"; both are Garmin statements and the article can cite both).
- *Five reasons.* Demanding routines ("experiences that feel good can also drain your battery"); inadequate sleep ("There is a huge difference between 90 or 60 in the morning"); fitness not keeping up (VO2 max framing, cites Gerber et al. 2013 and Teisala et al. 2014); alcohol ("Alcohol is a huge stressor for your body ... One recent study show that even a single drink can have a negative impact on the restorative quality of sleep (Pietilä et al., 2018)"); individual variation ("driving a car can be a huge stressor for one person ... but for someone else it might be a relaxing moment"). The alcohol point cites the same Finnish study the site already uses (E15); Garmin's "even a single drink" is consistent with that study's low-dose group (up to 0.25 g/kg, about one US drink for a 70 kg adult) showing effects at P < .001, but Garmin gives no size and no link.
- *Framing.* "A healthy approach to life doesn't mean keeping your Body Battery full all-day long. It is supposed to diminish during the day." Directly answers the PAA "How do I get my Garmin body battery to 100%?" by reframing it; the article should answer the question and then make the same point with the source.
- *Limits.* No illness (that is a separate Garmin post, E4), no late-exercise timing, no not-worn-overnight case, no accuracy discussion, no mention of what Garmin has or has not published. Two of its three citations are not linked to the study's own page (Pietilä has no link at all).

**Sonar Health (position 10; E27).** What it answers and how well:
- *Definition and scale.* "Body Battery is Garmin's daily energy score, measured from 5 to 100, that uses heart rate variability, stress, sleep and activity data" and a four-band table (76 to 100 "High energy and strong recovery", 51 to 75, 26 to 50, 5 to 25) with "How To Use It" advice. The inputs and bands match Garmin's manual (E2); the "how to use" column is Sonar's editorial. It links the Garmin support FAQ for the inputs.
- *Drains and recharges.* A list (activity, stress "even from things that feel positive", poor sleep, alcohol linking Garmin's blog, illness "which triggers an immune response that shows up as elevated stress before you even feel sick"). The illness sentence overstates Garmin's own position: Garmin's sick post says "Early on, it is difficult to say if a low battery is because of illness or something else" (E4). Also: "Garmin has also noted that food intake and stimulants like caffeine do not directly factor into the score", consistent with E2.
- *Accuracy.* "It estimates your available energy from several signals rather than measuring it directly." Then: "A peer-reviewed study published in JMIR notes that composite scores like Body Battery rely on proprietary algorithms that aren't public". The linked paper (PMC12647429) is Rosenbach et al. 2025 in *Stress and Health*, not JMIR, and it validates Garmin's stress score against ECG HRV; it does not study Body Battery (E21). The direction of the point (the algorithm is proprietary) is fair; the attribution is wrong. Then a useful caveat: a low score "can also come down to something as simple as a gap in your overnight data", and a loose band makes HRV noisier (cites the5krunner, a blog).
- *Comparison table.* Body Battery vs Oura and Google Health Readiness, Polar Recovery Pro, Amazfit BioCharge, with Sonar's own "Energy Reserve" promoted in the paragraph. Nothing on Training Readiness or HRV Status, which is odd for a Garmin explainer, and nothing on whether any of these publish their formula.
- *FAQ.* "Does a low Body Battery mean I'm sick?" ("Not necessarily ... a sudden unexplained drop that sticks around for several days is worth paying attention to"); "Can I increase my Body Battery during the day?" ("Short naps, meditation, light walking and simply relaxing can nudge the number back up"); "Why does my Body Battery drop during a workout?"; "Do I need a Garmin watch?". These are the PAA questions in editorial form; the article needs to answer them with Garmin's sources rather than opinion.

**Shared gaps (the contribution space):**
1. **No page says which Garmin numbers are documented how.** Garmin publishes, in its own pages, that HRV Status is overnight RMSSD averaged across the whole sleep in 5-minute windows, that the baseline is a range (example 33 to 45 ms) built over about three weeks and strengthened with a few months of data, and that Training Readiness has six named inputs (E6, E8, E9).

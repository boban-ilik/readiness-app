### Passage-grounded benchmark

**Cleveland Clinic (about 2,400 words, position 2 / AI Overview source on both).** What it answers, by section: *What is OTS* ("a condition that happens when you exercise too often or too intensely for long enough that it starts to hurt your body"); *Types*, a three-stage model: "Stage 1 overtraining syndrome (functional overtraining)", "Stage 2 ... (sympathetic overtraining syndrome)", "Stage 3 ... (parasympathetic OTS)", with "Overtraining syndrome isn't always a progressive condition"; *How common*: "Studies estimate that around two-thirds of elite runners will experience it at some point. Around one-third of all runners (regardless of competitive status) experience it."; *Symptoms by stage* (stage 1: muscle pain, weight change, anxiety, poor sleep, colds; stage 2: insomnia, mood changes, "Tachycardia (an unusually fast heartbeat, more than 100 beats per minute at rest)", hypertension; stage 3: fatigue, depression, lost motivation, "Bradycardia ... lower than 60 beats per minute at rest"); *Diagnosis*: a three-question checklist ("a drop in your performance or ability, even after getting plenty of rest and recovery?", mood changes, other explanations) and "There's not one test that can confirm for sure that you have overtraining syndrome"; then a list of tests where "High urea, uric acid, ammonia or creatine kinase in your blood can be signs of OTS" and "Unusually high levels of cortisol in your pee can mean you have OTS"; *Treatment*: rest, "Reducing your training intensity and how often you do it (usually anywhere from 50% to 70%)", complete rest; *Duration*: "the earlier stages usually last for around few weeks. Strengths: the only selected page with a diagnosis-by-exclusion checklist, a duration section and a reference list. Weaknesses against primary sources: (1) the stage model and the tachycardia/bradycardia cutoffs are a popularisation of Kreher's table of "parasympathetic" and "sympathetic" alterations (E2) and the "classical concept of sympathetic versus parasympathetic OTS" that the consensus statement mentions in passing (E1); the consensus itself classifies by recovery time (FOR, NFOR, OTS), not by numbered stage, and ">100 bpm" and "<60 bpm" are clinical definitions of tachycardia and bradycardia, not overtraining thresholds. A resting heart rate under 60 is ordinary in trained people. (2) "two-thirds of elite runners" traces to retrospective career surveys of elite runners (60% of females, 64% of males reporting at least one episode) and "one-third of all runners" is the consensus statement's 33% career rate in nonelite adult runners (E1); the per-season rate the consensus gives is about 10% (range 7 to 21%). The page merges FOR, NFOR and OTS under "OTS". (3) The blood and urine markers are stated as signs of OTS, whereas the consensus and Kreher both say no biochemical marker is sensitive or specific (E1, E2, E31). **Shared gaps (the contribution space):**
1. **No page separates what a watch measures from what only the athlete notices.** HSS's three groups are the nearest attempt but mix the two (its "health-related" list has RHR beside appetite). The packet supports a clean split: measurable (RHR, HRV, sleep duration and efficiency, HR at a given pace) with the direction, size and reliability of each in controlled overload studies (E1, E3, E5, E6, E8, E10, E11, E32), versus felt (performance at a given effort, perceived effort, mood, motivation, appetite, soreness, illness frequency) where the research says subjective reports are the more sensitive signal (E12, E13, E1).
2. **No page says that the measured signs often go the "wrong" way or stay flat.** The meta-analysis of overreaching studies found resting HRV "largely unaffected", with a small increase in resting RMSSD when performance fell (E3); weekly-averaged RMSSD rose and resting HR fell in triathletes driven into functional overreaching (E6); a cyclist study found RMSSD "likely increased" after two weeks of heavy training (E8); the consensus says RHR changes "are not consistently found" in OTS (E1); the HR meta-analysis found a moderate RHR rise only after short (under two weeks) overload and none after longer blocks, with changes that "may fall within the day-to-day variability" (E32). This is the single most important correction to every competitor's "resting heart rate goes up" line and to Garmin's own "high HRV can mean overreach" note (E21).
3. **No page gives sizes.** The packet has them: RHR about 4.5 bpm higher on average after short overload (E1 reporting E32); sleep duration down 7.9% and efficiency down 1.6% in functionally overreached triathletes, URTI 67% vs 22% vs 11% (E10); sleep efficiency 83.1% to 77.8% over nine days of intensified training (E11); performance down 9.0% in the overreached group of one study (E6); day-to-day noise of about 10% for resting HR and about 12% for Ln rMSSD in short morning recordings, with smallest worthwhile changes of about 2% and 3% (E5). Readers can compare their own week to these.
4. **No page states the three time frames with their evidence quality.** FOR: days to weeks (Kreider definition adopted by the consensus; "possible to recover from a state of OR within a 2-wk period"); NFOR: weeks to months; OTS: "months or possibly years"; all three are consensus convention built mostly on overreaching studies, since OTS cannot be induced ethically and "only few data exist on OTS" (E1, E2, E15). Kreher's 14 to 21 days of rest as the FOR/NFOR boundary "by some" is also convention (E2). Competitors state "weeks to months" as fact.
5. **No page says plainly that no single marker is diagnostic and that the diagnosis is retrospective and by exclusion.** Cleveland comes closest, then undercuts it with a list of "signs" in blood and urine. The consensus: "none of them meet all the criteria", "the only certain sign is a decrease in performance", "the diagnosis of OTS can often only be made retrospectively", exclusion of thyroid, adrenal, diabetes, iron deficiency with anemia, infections, eating disorders (E1); hormones mostly normal at rest (E31).
6. **No page gives a usable self-check before the doctor.** Supported building blocks: the consensus checklist (unexplainable underperformance, persistent fatigue, increased sense of effort in training, sleep disorders; then exclusions) (E1); a performance test at a fixed effort and the "ready to resume" criterion of normal responses to hard training (E1 considerations, HSS claim); weekly averages of at least 3 to 4 readings rather than single days (E5, E6); MedlinePlus's lay rule of 1 to 2 weeks of rest before seeing a provider (E30, tertiary, label as such); the REDs boundary for anyone who is under-eating, has lost their period, or is losing weight (E16, E1).
7. **No page handles the device metrics honestly.** Garmin's HRV Status and Training Status labels (Unbalanced, Low, Overreaching, Unproductive, Strained) are seven-day-average conventions needing weeks of data (E19, E20, E21); Apple Watch reports SDNN from intermittent background readings and from Breathe sessions (E22); Oura's HRV Balance compares 14 days with two months (E23). All are vendor conventions, none validated as a diagnosis.

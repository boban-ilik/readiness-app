The number in your watch app is lower than yesterday's, or lower than last month's, or lower than the one your training partner keeps posting. Before working out why, settle one question: low compared with what? HRV means something against your own recent readings on the same device. Against someone else's number or an age chart, it mostly tells you about age, genes and which watch you wear.

Once you compare with yourself, start with the ordinary explanations, because they are the ones with measured effects. Of the causes measured in overnight or morning readings, alcohol and illness move the number the most, and both push resting heart rate up at the same time. That makes the heart-rate line in the same app the quickest way to narrow things down.

Below, nine causes are ranked by how big a drop each usually produces, with a plain "not measured" where no study has put a size on it. Seven are real dips. Two, age and the device, only make a number look low. After the ranking comes the part that decides what you do: whether you are looking at ordinary wobble, a dip with a cause, a trend worth acting on, or simply your normal.

## Low compared with what?

Your baseline is your typical reading over recent weeks, measured the same way each night. That is the only comparison that tells you whether today is low.

The metric matters too. Garmin, Oura and WHOOP report RMSSD, a measure of how much each heartbeat's timing differs from the one before, which mostly reflects the resting, recovery side of the nervous system. Apple Health reports SDNN, the overall spread of beat-to-beat intervals across a sample. They are different calculations, so a 35 on one is not a 35 on the other.

That is also the honest answer to "Is 20 normal?" or "Is an HRV of 17 bad?". It depends on the metric, the device and your age, and there is no official line. As the cardiologist Christopher P. Cannon writes for [Harvard Health Publishing](https://www.health.harvard.edu/heart-health/how-relevant-is-heart-rate-variability), there are "no guidelines or recommendations from professional cardiovascular societies" about HRV. If you want typical ranges by age and a decoder for what each device reports, [what counts as a good HRV](https://thereadiness.app/blog/what-is-a-good-hrv/) has both. This article does not repeat them.

## The nine causes, ranked by how much they usually move HRV

The order is approximate. Alcohol, illness and training sizes come from large sets of overnight or morning readings. Altitude comes from expedition studies and heat from lab experiments, which are not a night in your own bed. Sleep loss and stress have a clear direction but no measured size, so they sit at the bottom of the dips rather than being given a number they have not earned.

### Seven dips: something changed in your body

| Cause | Typical size | How long it lasts | What else you would notice |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Alcohol | About [2, 6 and 13 ms lower RMSSD](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5878366/) in the first three hours of sleep after low, moderate and high doses (4,098 people, against their own alcohol-free nights); about [3.3 to 3.8 ms lower per extra drink](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12970902/) (about 21,000 WHOOP members) | The night you drink; the studies measured that night | Resting heart rate up, about 2.4 to 2.8 beats per minute per extra drink in the WHOOP data |
| 2. Illness | About [10% lower during sickness](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8659706/) on average (9 million morning readings) | Days; can start before symptoms | Resting heart rate and breathing rate up |
| 3. Altitude | RMSSD lower in the [first week above 2,500 m](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12812737/), a standardized effect of about 0.5 (15 studies, 698 adults); no percentage available | The first week was measured; eased after coming back down in one small study | A trip to the mountains |
| 4. Heat | RMSSD about [76 ms vs 53 ms, roughly 31% to 34% lower](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8198543/), lying for 10 minutes at 35 °C vs 19 °C (12 young adults, lab) | While you are hot; the overnight effect of a warm bedroom has not been measured | A hot room or a sauna that evening |
| 5. Hard training | About 4.6% change on training days (same 9-million-reading dataset); vagal recovery takes [up to 24 h after easy, 24 to 48 h after threshold, 48 h or more after hard sessions](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-013-0083-4) | One to two days per session; longer through a hard block | A hard session in the last 48 hours |
| 6. Poor or short sleep | Not measured as a size; RMSSD [significantly lower after sleep deprivation](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12394884/) (11 trials, 549 people) | Not measured | A short night on the same device |
| 7. Psychological stress | Not measured as a size; lower parasympathetic HRV under induced stress in [most of 37 studies](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5900369/) | Not measured | A stressful stretch you can name |

### Two reasons a number looks low that are not dips

| Cause | Typical size | How long it lasts | What else you would notice |
|---|---|---|---|
| 8. Age | RMSSD at about [47% of its second-decade level by the sixth decade](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0735-1097(97)00554-8), then stable (260 people, 24-hour ECG) | Permanent and gradual | Nothing; your normal moves with you |
| 9. Device and metric | Not a change in your body; night-level error against ECG of about [6% to 16% depending on the device](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12367097/) | As long as you wear that device | A new watch, a new app, or a different metric |

Age and the device are not one-night events, but they can open a bigger gap than anything in the dips table. Losing roughly half your RMSSD between youth and middle age is a far larger difference than any dip in the table above.

### Alcohol

The doses in the Finnish study were measured in grams of alcohol per kilogram of body weight, and the effect was similar in active and sedentary people. In the WHOOP data, where the data and some of the authors are the company's own, drinking earlier in the day and sleeping longer afterwards each made the effect smaller. Turning those doses into drinks, and cutting the effect down, belongs to the guide on raising HRV linked at the end.

### Illness

A drop of around 10% with heart rate and breathing rate rising is the usual signature. The section on resting heart rate below covers what the timing studies found, and what they do not let a watch do.

### Altitude

A "standardized effect of about 0.5" means the average drop was about half the usual spread of RMSSD values in those studies, which researchers call a moderate effect. For a rough yardstick, [the 9-million-reading dataset](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8659706/) reported its effects in the same kind of unit: about 0.55 for heavy drinking, 0.47 for sickness and 0.36 for training days. The study designs differ, so treat this as a rough comparison, but a first week in the mountains sits in the same company as a heavy night out.

Three details from the same evidence are worth knowing. Trained and untrained people did not differ in the analysis, so fitness does not protect you. SDNN, the Apple Health metric, fell more above 3,500 m than below it. And the effect is not always a straight line: in [a trek study of 89 adults](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8995742/), RMSSD rose at 3,619 m before falling below sea-level values at 4,600 m and 5,140 m. Even a modest elevation can register: in [22 medical staff who moved to 1,400 m](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11663842/), several HRV measures fell in the first week and improved after they came back down.

### Heat

The 31% to 34% figure comes from 10 minutes lying in a 35 °C room, in 12 people. It tells you heat lowers HRV while you are hot, not what a warm bedroom does to an overnight average, which has not been measured. A [systematic review of 27 passive-heating studies](https://doi.org/10.1088/1361-6579/aca0d9) found whole-body heating reduced the vagal, recovery-side signal in 14 of them. Saunas show a drop during the session, about 62% in log-transformed RMSSD by the end of 30 minutes in [six trained cyclists](https://doi.org/10.1139/apnm-2017-0581), and a rise during the cool-down afterwards in [93 people with cardiovascular risk factors](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ctim.2019.06.011). Whether an evening sauna changes the overnight number was not measured in either.

### Hard training

A hard session is supposed to lower HRV for a day or two; that dip is the cost of the work, not a warning. Fitter people recovered faster in the same review. A block of hard weeks is different from one session: in [nine elite rowers before the 2012 Olympics](https://doi.org/10.1123/ijspp.2013-0497), weeks with more time at high intensity held the weekly average down, by a standardized effect of about 0.2. Whether to train on a low morning is its own decision, covered in [how to use HRV to decide whether to train](https://thereadiness.app/blog/hrv-training/).

### Poor sleep and stress

Both are real, and both lack a size. In the sleep-deprivation trials, RMSSD fell significantly while SDNN, the Apple Health metric, did not, so a short night may show less clearly on an Apple Watch than on a Garmin or Oura. Short and irregular sleep also makes nights jumpier, which matters for the wobble section below. If short nights are the pattern, [how much sleep recovery needs](https://thereadiness.app/blog/how-much-sleep-for-recovery/) covers the target. For stress, the review of 37 studies shows the direction clearly but nothing that converts a stressful week into milliseconds.

### Age

The 47% figure comes from 24-hour ECGs, and the same decline shows up in wearables: HRV fell with age across [8 million Fitbit users](https://doi.org/10.1016/s2589-7500(20)30246-6). The good-HRV guide linked above has the by-age ranges.

### Device and metric

Each device measures a different slice of your night, so the same body produces different numbers.

- **Apple Watch:** Apple Health records SDNN ([Apple's HealthKit documentation](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/healthkit/hkquantitytypeidentifier/heartratevariabilitysdnn)), from short samples taken while you are still, including during Breathe sessions in the Mindfulness app ([Apple's heart rate white paper](https://www.apple.com/health/pdf/Heart_Rate_Calorimetry_Activity_on_Apple_Watch_November_2024.pdf)).
- **Garmin:** RMSSD averaged across the whole sleep, from 5-minute windows ([Garmin's HRV Status explainer](https://www.garmin.com/en-US/garmin-technology/health-science/hrv-status/)). Garmin itself notes that differences in measurement timing and duration make comparisons across devices a challenge. Garmin does not send HRV to Apple Health, which is covered in [getting Garmin HRV into Apple Health](https://thereadiness.app/blog/garmin-hrv-apple-health/).
- **Oura:** the mean of five-minute samples across the night ([Oura's help page](https://support.ouraring.com/hc/en-us/articles/360025441974-Heart-Rate-Variability)).
- **WHOOP:** calculated during your deepest period of sleep ([WHOOP's explainer](https://www.whoop.com/us/en/thelocker/low-hrv/)).

Two consequences follow. Switching watches or apps is a measurement change, not a change in you, so the old baseline no longer applies. And device error is not small: against ECG over 536 nights in 13 adults, the [average night-level error](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12367097/) was about 6% to 7% for Oura, 8% for WHOOP, 10.5% for Garmin and 16% for Polar. A one-night drop smaller than that may simply be the device.

## The resting heart rate check

HRV on its own is quick to react and poor at naming the cause. The authors of the 9-million-reading analysis called it sensitive but not specific. Resting heart rate, recorded by the same watch, narrows it down.

In [that dataset](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8659706/), heart rate was about 6% higher after heavy drinking and about 6% higher during sickness, alongside HRV drops of about 12% and 10%. The link is mechanical: a faster heart leaves less time between beats, and so less room for the gaps to vary ([Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/symptoms/21773-heart-rate-variability-hrv)).

Use it like this:

- **HRV down, resting heart rate up, drinks last night.** Alcohol is the likeliest explanation, and a night without drinks is the simplest test.
- **HRV down, resting heart rate up, no drinks.** Illness moves to the top of the list, more so if breathing rate is up too. In [2,745 Fitbit users with COVID-19](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7705652/), heart rate and breathing rate were typically elevated by illness while HRV decreased. In a [Stanford smartwatch study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9020268/), 26 of 32 people with COVID-19 showed changes in heart rate, steps or sleep, and in 22 of the 25 with symptom records the change appeared before or at the start of symptoms; that detection relied on resting heart rate rising above each person's own baseline. On Apple Watch, the [daily rhythm of SDNN changed](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7901594/) in the week before a positive test in health care workers. None of this lets a watch diagnose illness or predict it a set number of days ahead. It does mean a low HRV with a raised resting heart rate and no other explanation is a reasonable cue to take it easy.
- **HRV down, resting heart rate normal.** The two causes known to lift heart rate are less likely, so look at the last 48 hours of training, sleep, stress, heat and travel. The research does not show that those causes leave heart rate untouched, so a normal heart rate is a pointer, not proof.
- **HRV low, resting heart rate low, and you are fit.** That pattern has its own explanation, covered under "your normal" below.

## Dip, trend, or your normal

This is where "Why has my HRV dropped?" and "Should I worry if my HRV is low?" get answered, because the answer depends on which of four things you are looking at.

### Ordinary wobble

HRV moves from night to night even when nothing has changed. In short morning recordings in athletes, [day-to-day variation in RMSSD](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3936188/) was about 12%. On an overnight wearable, [11 elite water polo players on WHOOP](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9505647/) showed within-week variation of about 5.4% over 16 weeks, calculated on the log scale researchers use for RMSSD. Device error, from the validation study above, is of the same order. Your own wobble is personal, and it takes data to see it: in [more than 21,000 wearable users](https://doi.org/10.1152/ajpheart.00738.2025), at least five of seven nights were needed to judge a week's variability reliably. The same analysis, by WHOOP-affiliated authors, found nights were jumpier with more alcohol, less activity, and shorter, less consistent sleep.

The review behind the 12% figure is also clear that no threshold for a meaningful change has been validated. So a single night 5% to 10% under your baseline, with nothing else going on, sits where noise, device error and real change overlap. It is not worth acting on alone.

### A dip

A dip is a drop you can name: one of the seven causes in the table, usually with the matching signal (raised heart rate, a hard session, a trip, a short night), followed by a return to your usual range as the cause passes. So when you ask "Why has my HRV dropped?", look at the last day or two first. If you can name it, there is nothing to fix beyond the cause itself.

### A trend

A trend is your weekly average sitting below your usual range for weeks, with no cause from the table to explain it. Judge it on weekly averages, not single mornings; in trained triathletes, [at least 3 readings a week](https://doi.org/10.1123/ijspp.2013-0455) were needed for a weekly average to hold up.

How long counts as "a while" is a product convention, not a research finding:

- **Garmin**, per the explainer linked above, builds a baseline range over about three weeks of overnight wear and compares your 7-day average with it, calling the status "low" when that average drops significantly below the range.
- **Polar** advises comparing with [your 28-day average](https://www.polar.com/en/guide/my-hrv-is-very-low).
- **Readiness Coach**, the app this site makes, uses a 30-day trimmed median as your baseline once it has 7 nights.

Treat all three as conventions. As the wobble section noted, research has not validated a threshold for a meaningful change, so none of these windows is the proven one.

What a real trend can look like is documented in one case. In [two elite triathletes followed for 77 days](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00421-012-2354-4), the 7-day rolling average of the athlete who became non-functionally overreached (training had outrun recovery long enough to hurt performance) slid week by week toward race day, while the other athlete's stayed flat. That athlete's day-to-day wobble also shrank. It is one case, not a rule. A [meta-analysis of 24 studies in endurance athletes](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-016-0484-2) found that when performance fell, resting RMSSD rose slightly on average rather than falling. So a falling trend is a prompt to look at sleep, illness, alcohol and training load together, not a diagnosis of overtraining, and a steady number does not prove all is well.

### Your normal

A number that is low but stable for weeks, in someone who feels well, with nothing in the dips table to explain it, can simply be where your number sits. It is not by itself a reason to worry, and there is no official cutoff to fall below.

Several things set where that is. Age is one, as the table showed. Genes matter: about [18% of the variation in RMSSD was heritable](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7480943/) in 149,067 people with 10-second ECGs, and [40% to 48%](https://doi.org/10.1161/01.cir.0000146334.96820.6e) in 772 twins and siblings with 24-hour recordings. The metric matters, as the device section showed.

**"I'm fit, so why is my HRV so low?"** Because fitness is not the main thing the number tracks. Across [28,175 app users](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8659706/), physical activity level was only weakly related to HRV (a correlation of 0.21), while age was more strongly related. Very fit people also run into a ceiling. In [24-hour ECG recordings](https://doi.org/10.1152/ajpheart.00433.2004), the vagal signal stopped rising as the heart slowed in 35 of 76 healthy people, an effect called saturation (measured as high-frequency power rather than RMSSD). In elite endurance athletes, [researchers describe](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-013-0071-8) HRV falling even as resting heart rate falls and fitness improves. A low HRV with a low resting heart rate in a fit person can be that ceiling, not a problem.

## When to see a doctor

Symptoms are the reason to get help, not the number. Cleveland Clinic's guidance on [heart palpitations](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/symptoms/17084-heart-palpitations) says to call 911 or your local emergency number if palpitations will not stop, or if you pass out, have more intense pain, pressure or tightness in your chest, neck, jaw, arms or upper back, or have trouble breathing. It advises contacting your provider if palpitations come with dizziness, confusion or light-headedness, shortness of breath or unusual sweating, or get worse or more frequent. A watch HRV reading does not detect any of these, so a normal number is no reason to ignore them.

Without symptoms, the case for a doctor's visit is a weekly average well below your usual range for weeks, with no cause from the table, while you feel unwell or run down. Bring the trend, not one morning.

The research linking low HRV with heart disease risk used ECG recordings across large populations. It does not turn one watch reading into a diagnosis; the good-HRV guide linked near the top covers that research and its limits.

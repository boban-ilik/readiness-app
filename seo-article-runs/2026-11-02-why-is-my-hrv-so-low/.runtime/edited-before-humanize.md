The number in your watch app is lower than yesterday's, or lower than last month's, or lower than the one your training partner keeps posting. Before working out why, settle one question: low compared with what? HRV means something against your own recent readings on the same device. Against someone else's number or an age chart, it mostly tells you about age, genes and which watch you wear.

Once you compare with yourself, start with the ordinary explanations, because they are the ones with measured effects. Of the causes measured in overnight or morning readings, alcohol and illness move the number the most, and both push resting heart rate up at the same time. That makes the heart-rate line in the same app the quickest way to tell them apart from the rest.

Below, nine causes are ranked by how big a drop each usually produces, with a plain "not measured" where no study has put a size on it. Seven are real dips; age and the device only make a number look low. Then comes the part that decides what you do: ordinary wobble, a dip with a cause, a trend worth acting on, or simply your normal.

## Low compared with what?

Your baseline is your typical reading over recent weeks, measured the same way each night. It is the only comparison that tells you whether today is low.

The metric matters too. Garmin, Oura and WHOOP report RMSSD, a measure of how much each heartbeat's timing differs from the one before, which mostly reflects the resting, recovery side of the nervous system. Apple Health reports SDNN, the overall spread of beat-to-beat intervals across a sample. They are different calculations, so a 35 on one is not a 35 on the other.

That is also the honest answer to "Is 20 normal?" or "Is an HRV of 17 bad?". It depends on the metric, the device and your age, and there is no official line. As the cardiologist Christopher P. Cannon writes for [Harvard Health Publishing](https://www.health.harvard.edu/heart-health/how-relevant-is-heart-rate-variability), there are "no guidelines or recommendations from professional cardiovascular societies" about HRV. Typical ranges by age and a decoder for what each device reports are in [what counts as a good HRV](https://thereadiness.app/blog/what-is-a-good-hrv/); this article does not repeat them.

## The nine causes, ranked by how much they usually move HRV

The order is approximate, because the studies use different units and settings. Alcohol, illness and training sizes come from large sets of overnight or morning readings. Altitude comes from studies of people taken up to altitude and heat from a lab experiment, and neither is a night in your own bed. Sleep loss and stress have a clear direction but no measured size, so they sit at the bottom of the dips rather than being given a number they have not earned.

### Seven dips: something changed in your body

| Cause | Typical size | How long it lasts | What else you would notice |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Alcohol | About 12% lower the morning after heavy drinking ([9 million morning readings](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8659706/)); about [2, 6 and 13 ms lower RMSSD](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5878366/) in the first three hours of sleep after low, moderate and high doses (4,098 people, against their own alcohol-free nights) | The night you drink; that is the night the studies measured | Resting heart rate up |
| 2. Illness | About 10% lower during sickness, in the [same 9 million readings](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8659706/) | Days; changes can show up before a positive test | Resting heart rate and breathing rate up |
| 3. Altitude | RMSSD lower in the [first week above 2,500 m](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12812737/) (about 8,200 ft), a moderate standardized effect of about 0.5 (15 studies, 698 adults); no percentage available | The first week was measured; eased after coming back down in one small study | A trip to the mountains |
| 4. Heat | RMSSD [about 76 ms vs 53 ms](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8198543/), roughly 31% to 34% lower, lying for 10 minutes at 35 °C (95 °F) vs 19 °C (66 °F) (12 young adults, lab) | While you are hot; the overnight effect of a warm bedroom has not been measured | A hot room or a sauna that evening |
| 5. Hard training | About a 4.6% change on training days ([same 9 million readings](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8659706/)); vagal recovery takes [up to 24 h after easy, 24 to 48 h after threshold, 48 h or more after hard sessions](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-013-0083-4) | One to two days per session; longer through a hard block | A hard session in the last 48 hours |
| 6. Poor or short sleep | Not measured as a size; RMSSD [significantly lower after sleep deprivation](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12394884/) (11 trials, 549 people) | Not measured | A short night on the same device |
| 7. Psychological stress | Not measured as a size; HRV changed under induced stress in [most of 37 studies](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5900369/), most often as lower parasympathetic activity | Not measured | A stressful stretch you can name |

### Two reasons a number looks low that are not dips

| Cause | Typical size | How long it lasts | What else you would notice |
|---|---|---|---|
| 8. Age | RMSSD at [about 47% of its level at age 10 to 19 by age 50 to 59](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0735-1097%2897%2900554-8), then stable (260 people, 24-hour ECG) | Permanent and gradual | Nothing; your normal moves with you |
| 9. Device and metric | Not a change in your body; [average night-level error against ECG](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12367097/) of about 6% to 16%, depending on the device | As long as you wear that device | A new watch, a new app, or a different metric |

Neither is a one-night event, and both can explain a gap that no bad night would. Losing roughly half your RMSSD between your teens and your fifties is a bigger gap than any dip measured above, and a different device or metric puts the same night on a different scale.

### Altitude

A "standardized effect of about 0.5" means the average drop was about half the usual spread of RMSSD values in those studies, which researchers class as moderate. The meta-analysis did not report it in milliseconds or percent, so there is no honest way to say what it means for your watch's number.

Three details matter for a trip. Fitness does not protect you: trained and untrained people did not differ. SDNN, the Apple Health metric, [fell more above 3,500 m](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12812737/) (about 11,500 ft) than below it. Modest heights can register too: in [22 medical staff who moved to 1,400 m](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11663842/) (about 4,600 ft), several HRV measures fell in the first week and improved after they came back down.

### Heat

The lab figure shows that heat lowers HRV while you are hot, not what a warm bedroom does overnight. A [systematic review of 27 passive-heating studies](https://doi.org/10.1088/1361-6579/aca0d9) points the same way: whole-body heating reduced the vagal, recovery-side signal in 14 studies. Sauna results are mixed: HRV fell sharply during a 30-minute session in [six trained cyclists](https://doi.org/10.1139/apnm-2017-0581) and rose during the cool-down in [93 people with cardiovascular risk factors](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ctim.2019.06.011). Neither study measured the overnight number after an evening sauna.

### Hard training

A hard session should lower HRV for a day or two, and the harder the session, the longer the dip lasts; fitter people recovered faster in the same review. A block of hard weeks is different from one session: in [nine elite rowers in the 26 weeks before the 2012 Olympics](https://doi.org/10.1123/ijspp.2013-0497), weeks with more time at high intensity held the weekly average down, by a small standardized effect of about 0.2. Whether to train on a low morning is its own decision, covered in [how to use HRV to decide whether to train](https://thereadiness.app/blog/hrv-training/).

### Poor sleep and stress

Both are real and both lack a size. The sleep evidence comes from deprivation experiments, not ordinary short nights at home, so it gives the direction and nothing about how many milliseconds one late night costs. Short and irregular sleep also makes nights jumpier, which matters for the wobble section below. If short nights are the pattern, [how much sleep recovery needs](https://thereadiness.app/blog/how-much-sleep-for-recovery/) covers the target.

### Device and metric

Each device measures a different slice of time, so the same body produces different numbers. Apple Watch reports SDNN from short samples taken while you are still, including during Breathe sessions in the Mindfulness app ([Apple's heart rate white paper](https://www.apple.com/health/pdf/Heart_Rate_Calorimetry_Activity_on_Apple_Watch_November_2024.pdf)), so a day with a Breathe session and a day without are not quite the same measurement. Garmin averages RMSSD across the whole sleep from five-minute windows ([Garmin's HRV Status explainer](https://www.garmin.com/en-US/garmin-technology/health-science/hrv-status/)). Oura reports the mean of five-minute samples across the night ([Oura's help page](https://support.ouraring.com/hc/en-us/articles/360025441974-Heart-Rate-Variability)), and WHOOP says it calculates HRV during your deepest period of sleep. If you wear a Garmin but read your data in Apple Health, Garmin does not send HRV there; [getting Garmin HRV into Apple Health](https://thereadiness.app/blog/garmin-hrv-apple-health/) covers the workaround.

Two consequences follow. Switching watches or apps is a measurement change, not a change in you, so the old baseline no longer applies. And device error is not small: in the [validation study in the table](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12367097/) (13 adults, 536 nights), average night-level error against ECG was about 6% to 7% for Oura, 8% for WHOOP, 10.5% for Garmin and 16% for Polar. A one-night drop smaller than that may simply be the device.

## The resting heart rate check

HRV on its own reacts quickly but is poor at naming the cause; the authors of the 9-million-reading analysis called it sensitive but not specific. Resting heart rate, recorded by the same watch, narrows it down.

In [that dataset](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8659706/), heart rate was about 6% higher after heavy drinking and about 6% higher during sickness, alongside the HRV drops of about 12% and 10% in the table. The link is mechanical: a faster heart leaves less time between beats, and so less room for the gaps to vary.

Use it like this:

- **HRV down, resting heart rate up, drinks last night.** Alcohol is the likeliest explanation, and a night without drinks is the simplest test. Even a small change shows: in [about 21,000 WHOOP members](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12970902/) (WHOOP's data, some WHOOP authors), one drink above a person's usual average versus one below came with HRV about 3.3 to 3.8 ms lower and resting heart rate about 2.4 to 2.8 beats per minute higher.
- **HRV down, resting heart rate up, no drinks.** Illness moves to the top of the list, more so if breathing rate is up too. A low HRV with a raised resting heart rate and no other explanation is a reasonable cue to take it easy.
- **HRV down, resting heart rate normal.** The two causes known to lift heart rate are less likely, so look at the last 48 hours of training, sleep, stress, heat and travel. The research does not show that those causes leave heart rate untouched, so a normal heart rate is a pointer, not proof.
- **HRV low, resting heart rate low, and you are fit.** That pattern has its own explanation, covered under "your normal" below.

The illness studies support that cue and no more. In [2,745 Fitbit users with COVID-19](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7705652/), heart rate and breathing rate were typically raised by illness while HRV decreased. In a [Stanford smartwatch study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9020268/), 26 of 32 infected people showed changes in heart rate, steps or sleep, and in 22 of the 25 with symptom records the change appeared before or at the start of symptoms; that detection relied on resting heart rate rising above each person's own baseline. On Apple Watch, the [daily rhythm of SDNN changed](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7901594/) in the week before a positive test in health care workers. None of this lets a watch diagnose illness or predict it a set number of days ahead.

## Dip, trend, or your normal

Plain answers first. **Why has my HRV dropped?** Usually because of something from the table in the last day or two, and the resting heart rate check narrows down which. **Should I worry if my HRV is low?** Not about one low morning, and not about a number that is low but stable while you feel well. Weeks below your usual range with no cause, or any of the symptoms in the doctor section below, deserve attention.

### Ordinary wobble

HRV moves from night to night even when nothing has changed. In short morning recordings in athletes, [day-to-day variation in RMSSD](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3936188/) was about 12%. On an overnight wearable, [11 elite water polo players on WHOOP](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9505647/) varied by about 5.4% within a week over 16 weeks, on the log scale researchers use for RMSSD. Device error is of the same order. Your own wobble is personal, and it takes data to see it: in [more than 21,000 wearable users](https://doi.org/10.1152/ajpheart.00738.2025), at least five of seven nights were needed to judge a week's variability reliably. The same analysis, by WHOOP-affiliated authors, found nights were jumpier with more alcohol, less activity, and shorter, less consistent sleep.

The review behind the 12% figure also says no threshold for a meaningful change has been validated. As a rule of thumb rather than a research threshold: a single night 5% to 10% under your baseline, with nothing else going on, sits where noise, device error and real change overlap, and is not worth acting on alone.

### A dip

A dip is a drop you can name: one of the seven causes in the table, usually with the matching signal (raised heart rate, a hard session, a trip, a short night), followed by a return to your usual range as the cause passes. If you can name it, there is nothing to fix beyond the cause itself.

### A trend

A trend is your weekly average sitting below your usual range for weeks, with no cause from the table to explain it. Judge it on weekly averages, not single mornings; in trained triathletes, [at least 3 readings a week](https://doi.org/10.1123/ijspp.2013-0455) were needed for a weekly average to hold up.

How many weeks counts is a product convention, not a research finding, and none of these windows has been validated:

- **Garmin**, per its [HRV Status explainer](https://www.garmin.com/en-US/garmin-technology/health-science/hrv-status/), builds a baseline range over about three weeks of overnight wear and calls your status "low" when your 7-day average drops significantly below that range.
- **Polar's** guide to low HRV advises comparing your score only with your 28-day average.
- **Readiness Coach**, the app this site makes, uses a 30-day trimmed median as your baseline once it has 7 nights.

One documented case shows what a real trend can look like. In [two elite triathletes followed for 77 days](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00421-012-2354-4), the 7-day rolling average of the athlete who became non-functionally overreached (training had outrun recovery long enough to hurt performance) slid week by week toward race day, and their day-to-day wobble shrank, while the other athlete's average stayed flat. It is one case, and the wider evidence cuts both ways: a [meta-analysis of 24 studies in endurance athletes](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-016-0484-2) found that when performance fell, resting RMSSD on average rose slightly rather than falling. So a falling trend is a prompt to look at sleep, illness, alcohol and training load together, not a diagnosis of overtraining, and a steady number does not prove all is well.

### Your normal

A number that is low but stable for weeks, in someone who feels well, with nothing in the dips table to explain it, can simply be where your number sits.

Age and the metric set part of where that is, as the table showed. Genes set more of it: about [18% of the variation in RMSSD was heritable](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7480943/) in 149,067 people with 10-second ECGs, and [40% to 48%](https://doi.org/10.1161/01.cir.0000146334.96820.6e) in 772 twins and siblings with 24-hour recordings.

**"I'm fit, so why is my HRV so low?"** Because fitness is not the main thing the number tracks. Among the [28,175 users in the 9-million-reading dataset](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8659706/), physical activity level was only weakly related to HRV (a correlation of 0.21), while older age went with lower HRV more strongly. Very fit people can also hit a ceiling. In [24-hour ECG recordings](https://doi.org/10.1152/ajpheart.00433.2004), the vagal signal stopped rising as the heart slowed in 35 of 76 healthy people, an effect called saturation (measured as high-frequency power rather than RMSSD). And in elite endurance athletes, [researchers describe](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-013-0071-8) HRV falling even as resting heart rate falls and fitness improves. A low HRV with a low resting heart rate in a fit person can be that ceiling, not a problem.

## When to see a doctor

Symptoms are the reason to get help, not the number. Cleveland Clinic's guidance on [heart palpitations](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/symptoms/17084-heart-palpitations) says to call 911 or your local emergency number if palpitations will not stop, or if you pass out, have more intense pain, pressure or tightness in your chest, neck, jaw, arms or upper back, or have trouble breathing. It says palpitations may point to a more serious problem if they come with dizziness, confusion or light-headedness, chest pain, shortness of breath or unusual sweating, and to call your provider if they get worse or happen more often. A watch HRV reading does not detect any of these, so a normal number is no reason to ignore them.

Without symptoms, the case for a doctor's visit is a weekly average well below your usual range for weeks, with no cause from the table, while you feel unwell or run down. Bring the trend, not one morning.

The research linking low HRV with heart disease risk used ECG recordings in large populations, not watch readings, so it does not turn one low number into a diagnosis; the good-HRV guide linked near the top covers that research and its limits.
